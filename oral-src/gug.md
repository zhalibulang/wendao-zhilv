# The Forbidden City

Full-Route English Guide Script

## 1. Welcome, Name, and History

Good morning, everyone! Welcome to the Forbidden City. My name is Yupeng, and I will be your guide today.

紫禁城名称的由来

In Chinese, the Forbidden City is called Zijin Cheng, meaning the “Purple Forbidden City.” The word “forbidden” reflects the restricted access to the imperial palace.

In ancient Chinese astronomy, the region around the North Star was called the Purple Enclosure.

It was regarded as the residence of the Heavenly Emperor.

The emperor was the Son of Heaven, so his palace on earth mirrored that heavenly one.

建筑年代

Construction of the Forbidden City began in 1406 and was completed in 1420, during the reign of the Yongle Emperor of the Ming Dynasty.

迁都原因

In 1421, the emperor formally moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing.

First, Beijing was strategically important for defending China's northern frontier.

Second, before he became emperor, Zhu Di had been the Prince of Yan, with his power base in Beijing.

In 1402, he had seized the throne from his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor.

For 491 years, from 1421 to 1912, fourteen Ming and ten Qing emperors ruled from here.

故宫博物院

The last Qing emperor, Puyi, was forced to leave the Forbidden City in 1924.

The following year, on October 10, 1925, the complex was formally opened to the public as the Palace Museum.

In 1987, the Forbidden City was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

## 2. Overall Layout

The Forbidden City covers approximately 72 hectares.

It measures about 961 meters from north to south and 753 meters from east to west.

It is enclosed by a wall about ten meters high and a moat approximately 52 meters wide.

Each corner of the wall has an elaborate watchtower, traditionally described as having “nine beams, eighteen pillars, and seventy-two ridges.”

There are four main gates.

The Meridian Gate stands to the south,
the Gate of Divine Prowess to the north,
the East Glorious Gate to the east,
and the West Glorious Gate to the west.

The palace complex follows the traditional principle of “the Outer Court in front and the Inner Court behind.”

The Outer Court was mainly used for state ceremonies and official affairs, while the Inner Court contained the emperor's living quarters and the residences of the imperial family.

The main buildings are arranged along a north–south central axis, with important compounds extending to either side.

We will follow this axis from south to north, starting at the Meridian Gate, then the Gate of Supreme Harmony, the Three Great Halls, the Three Rear Palaces, and the Imperial Garden, and ending at the Gate of Divine Prowess.

## 3. The Meridian Gate

The Meridian Gate is also known as the Five-Phoenix Tower (五凤楼), named for the five pavilions on top.

The central passageway was reserved solely for the emperor. Officials used the passageway on the left, while members of the imperial family used the right one.

## 4. The Three Great Halls

After passing through the Meridian Gate, we enter the main ceremonial area of the palace.

The Inner Golden Water River curves like a bow, and the five white-marble bridges cross it.

Ahead stands the Gate of Supreme Harmony, which leads to the Three Great Halls of the Outer Court.

On the eastern and western sides of the Outer Court are the Hall of Literary Glory and the Hall of Martial Spirit.

Gate of Supreme Harmony

Passing through the Gate of Supreme Harmony, we come to the first hall of the Three Great Halls.

All three halls stand on a three-tiered white-marble platform.

The platform is more than 8 meters high at the center.

It is surrounded by carved marble balustrades decorated with dragon and phoenix designs.

Beneath the balustrades are 1,142 carved marble dragon-head spouts that drain rainwater.

During heavy rain, water pours from their mouths, creating the effect known as “a thousand dragons spouting water.”

The Hall of Supreme Harmony (太和殿)

The Hall of Supreme Harmony is the most dignified building in the Forbidden City and is commonly referred to as the Hall of Golden Chimes

It was used for the grandest occasions: the emperor's enthronement, his wedding, the New Year audience, and the sending of generals to war.

In front of the hall is a broad platform known as the Moon Terrace. It holds a sundial, a standard grain measure, a pair of bronze turtles, a pair of bronze cranes, and eighteen bronze vessels. The sundial and grain measure stand for imperial justice; the tortoises and cranes, for longevity.

The central marble ramp, carved with dragons and clouds, was reserved for the emperor's sedan chair.

The hall is eleven bays wide and five bays deep.

It is 26.92 meters high, with a total height of 35.05 meters including the platform.

Its roof is a double-eaved hip roof, the highest-ranking roof form in traditional Chinese architecture.

Ten glazed mythical beasts line each corner ridge, more than on any other building.

Inside, the floor is paved with golden bricks.

The gilded throne, carved with nine dragons, stands on a red-sandalwood platform in the central bay.

Six massive columns decorated with gilded coiling dragons flank the throne, and a gilded screen carved with dragons stands behind it.

Four pairs of ceremonial objects are arranged in front of the throne: elephants, luduan, cranes, and incense pavilions.

The elephants symbolize peace; the luduan, a mythical beast, the obedience of distant lands; the cranes, longevity.

Above the throne is a caisson ceiling.

At its center, a coiling dragon reaches downward, holding a pearl in its mouth.

The Hall of Middle Harmony

Behind the Hall of Supreme Harmony stands the Hall of Middle Harmony, a small square hall.

It has a single-eaved pyramidal roof.

Inside, a throne stands in the center, with two imperial sedan chairs beside it.

Before major ceremonies at the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the emperor rested here and received formal obeisance from officials.

Before conducting sacrificial rites in person, he reviewed the sacrificial texts here.

Before the spring ploughing ceremony, he inspected seeds and farming implements.

The Hall of Preserving Harmony

The third hall is the Hall of Preserving Harmony.

It is nine bays wide and five bays deep and stands 29.5 meters high.

Its roof is a double-eaved hip-and-gable roof.

Nine glazed animal figurines stand at the corners of both the upper and lower eaves.

A throne facing south stands on the central axis.

Six columns are absent from the front section, leaving a broader interior.

In the Ming Dynasty, the emperor changed into ceremonial robes here before major ceremonies.

In the Qing Dynasty, the hall hosted banquets on New Year's Eve for princes and high-ranking officials.

From 1789 onward, the Palace Examination — the highest level of the imperial civil service examinations — was held here every three years.

Marble Ramp Carved with Clouds and Dragons

Behind the Hall of Preserving Harmony lies a marble ramp carved with mountain cliffs, waves, clouds, and nine dragons.

It is more than sixteen meters long and weighs approximately 200 metric tons. It was dragged from a quarry seventy kilometers away over an ice road in winter.

## 5. The Three Rear Palaces

Now we leave the Outer Court, where the emperor governed, and enter the Inner Court, his private world.

Behind the Gate of Heavenly Purity stand the three main buildings of the Inner Court:

the Palace of Heavenly Purity,
the Hall of Union,
and the Palace of Earthly Tranquility.

The Palace of Heavenly Purity

The Palace of Heavenly Purity is nine bays wide and five bays deep, the largest of the three.

It served as the emperor's main residence during the Ming Dynasty and the early Qing Dynasty. From the reign of the Yongzheng Emperor onward, the emperor's residence shifted to the Hall of Mental Cultivation. After that, it was used for audiences, banquets, and mourning rites.

Above the throne hangs a plaque in the Shunzhi Emperor's handwriting: “Open and Aboveboard” (正大光明).

Beginning with the Yongzheng Emperor, the designated successor's name was written on two matching documents.

One was sealed in the box behind the plaque, while the other was kept by the emperor. After the emperor's death, the documents were compared to identify the chosen successor.

The Hall of Union

Next is the Hall of Union.

It was where the empress received greetings from the imperial concubines on her birthday and major festivals.

In 1748, the Qianlong Emperor placed the twenty-five imperial seals that represented imperial authority in this hall.

The mechanical striking clock and the copper clepsydra (a water clock) installed in the hall set the standard for timekeeping throughout the Forbidden City.

A plaque inscribed with the philosophical maxim “Non-interference” is hung above the throne.

The Shunzhi Emperor also placed an iron signboard in this hall bearing the admonition: “Eunuchs are forbidden to interfere with political affairs.”

The Palace of Earthly Tranquility

Finally, we come to the Palace of Earthly Tranquility.

The Palace of Earthly Tranquility served both as a hall for shamanic rituals and as the imperial bridal chamber. The bridal chamber is painted red and hung with “double happiness” characters.

It served as the bridal chamber for Emperors Kangxi, Tongzhi, Guangxu, as well as for the abdicated Xuantong Emperor.

## 6. The Hall of Mental Cultivation

Now the story turns. For two centuries, the real center of power was not in these grand halls, but in a smaller building nearby.

To the west of the central axis stands the Hall of Mental Cultivation.

Built in the Ming Dynasty, it became the emperors' residence and office from the Yongzheng Emperor onward. Eight Qing emperors lived and worked here in succession.

The main complex has a floor plan shaped like the Chinese character gong, roughly resembling the letter “I.” The front hall was for state affairs, and the rear hall was the emperor's bedchamber.

The emperor's throne stands at the center of the main hall.

Above it hangs a plaque bearing the words "Just and Benevolent" written in Emperor Yongzheng's own hand.

The main hall was used to receive officials, read memorials, and handle state affairs.

In the West Warm Chamber, a small study contains a plaque inscribed with “Diligent in Affairs, Respectful to the Talented”.

The room was screened off from the main hall, allowing him to read reports and meet ministers in private.

The inner room in the Western Chamber is known as Sanxitang, or the Room of Three Rare Treasures.

Its name comes from three celebrated works of Jin-dynasty calligraphy treasured by Emperor Qianlong:

“Clear Sky After Snow” by Wang Xizhi,
“Mid-Autumn” by Wang Xianzhi,
and “Boyuantie” by Wang Xun

Today, “Clear Sky After Snow” is in the National Palace Museum in Taipei, while the other two are in the Palace Museum in Beijing.

The East Warm Chamber is where “holding court behind the curtain” took place. From 1861, the Empress Dowagers Ci'an and Cixi ruled from here on behalf of the young Tongzhi Emperor. Cixi later did the same for the Guangxu Emperor.

Here, on February 12, 1912, Empress Dowager Longyu issued the abdication edict in the name of the young Xuantong Emperor, Puyi. The edict brought the imperial system to an end.

## 7. The Twelve Palaces and the Outer Routes

On either side of the Inner Court are the Six Eastern Palaces and the Six Western Palaces, which served as residences for imperial consorts.

The Six Western Palaces are

the Palace of Eternal Longevity,
the Hall of Great Supremacy,
the Palace of Eternal Spring,
the Palace of Assisting the Empress,
the Palace of Gathered Elegance,
and the Palace of Universal Happiness.

Gathered Elegance, where she gave birth to the future Tongzhi Emperor.-|A}

The Six Eastern Palaces are

the Palace of Great Benevolence,
the Palace of Inheriting Heaven,
the Palace of Gathering Essence,
the Palace of Prolonging Happiness,
the Palace of Eternal Harmony,
and the Palace of Great Sunlight.

Beyond these lie two further areas: the Outer Eastern Route and the Outer Western Route.

The Outer Eastern Route includes the Nine Dragon Screen and the Palace of Tranquil Longevity.

It was built for the Qianlong Emperor's retirement, a miniature palace with its own halls and garden.

The area also contains the Hall of Imperial Supremacy and the Changyin Pavilion, the largest theater in the palace.

The Outer Western Route includes the Palace of Benevolent Tranquility, the residence of the empress dowagers.

## 8. The Imperial Garden

After so much grandeur, let us slow down. At the northern end of the central axis lies the Imperial Garden, covering approximately 12,000 square meters.

At its center stands the Hall of Imperial Peace (钦安殿), the only religious building on the central axis. It houses a Taoist image of Zhenwu, the God of Water, believed to protect the palace from fire.

In front of the hall stand the Intertwined Cypresses, four hundred years old.

The garden also features rockeries made from Taihu stone, including Duixiu Hill, which has a pavilion at its summit.

Other landmarks include the Pavilion of Myriad Springtimes and the Pavilion of One Thousand Autumns.

More than nine hundred mosaic scenes appear along the pebble paths.

## 9. The Gate of Divine Prowess and Closing

We now reach the northern gate, the Gate of Divine Prowess (神武门). It was formerly known as Xuanwu Gate.

Under the Kangxi Emperor, whose personal name was Xuanye, it was renamed to avoid the character “Xuan” in his name. The same taboo changed the god's name in the garden: Xuanwu became Zhenwu.

I hope this visit has helped you appreciate the scale and beauty of the Forbidden City. Thank you for joining me today.
